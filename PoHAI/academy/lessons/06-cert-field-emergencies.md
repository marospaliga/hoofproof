# Certification lesson 6 — Field emergencies

Emergencies are where documentation discipline either holds or collapses.
This lesson covers the situations where minutes matter — and where your job
as a documenter is to *enable* professionals, not replace them.

## Triage: what kills fastest

Rank threats by how quickly they kill, in this order:

1. **Dystocia (calving trouble)** — a cow straining >30 minutes with no
   progress, or >2 hours since waters broke with no calf, is an emergency.
   First check position: two front hooves, nose between them, is normal.
   Anything else (a tail first, a head back, one hoof) is malpresented — vet
   now. *Never* pull blindly; you can kill cow and calf.
2. **Milk fever / down cow** — cannot stand after calving. Calcium emergency.
   Do not drag her by the udder. Vet treatment (calcium borogluconate) works
   dramatically fast when given early; late treatment often fails.
3. **Bloat** — left flank swollen tight like a drum, cow distressed,
   stamping. Free-gas bloat from feedlot grain is a vet emergency (trocar or
   lavage). Pasture frothy bloat (legumes, lush grass) — walk the cow slowly
   (never run), keep her head up, call the vet. A struggling, bloaty cow can
   die within an hour.
4. **Severe prolapse** — vaginal after calving: keep the cow lying on a
   slight decline so gravity helps, cover with clean moist cloth, vet soon.
   Rectal prolapse: less urgent but still needs attention.
5. **Trauma** — caught in gates, fallen in a ditch, gored. Control bleeding
   with direct pressure; immobilize; vet for anything deep or large.

## Your role in an emergency: the documenter

While someone else handles the animal (or until they arrive):

- **Time everything.** Onset, actions taken, response. Vet treatment
  decisions depend on the timeline you give them.
- **Photo/video from a safe distance.** Do not stress or corner a distressed
  animal to get a better shot. A shaky 10-second video of breathing pattern
  beats nothing.
- **Record what was given, by whom, when.** Drug name, dose, route, time.
  This is both vet-critical and the exact content labor attestations are
  built from.
- **Post the outcome.** The follow-up — "stood after calcium, eating by
  evening" — is the evidence that closes the loop and lets raters judge
  response quality.

## Safety of the human

You are part of the protection system. A panicked large animal is more
dangerous than any disease on this list. Do not enter a pen with a distressed
cow alone, do not work between a cow and a hard object, and do not handle a
down large animal without help. Calling for help *is* the correct first
action — no evidence is worth an injury.

## Calm is a skill

The most useful person in a field emergency is the one whose hands don't
shake and whose voice stays flat. If you practice the triage list — read it
again after this lesson — you will recognize the emergency instead of
freezing on it. Recognition is half of response.

## Quiz

1. A cow has been straining for 40 minutes with no calf visible. You:
   - a) Pull hard when you see hooves
   - b) Check position; anything other than two front hooves + nose means
     call the vet immediately; don't pull blindly ✓
   - c) Wait an hour — calving takes time
2. A cow is down, cannot stand, hours after calving. First response:
   - a) Drag her to the barn by the udder
   - b) Keep her comfortable, call the vet for calcium treatment, note the
     timeline of when she went down ✓
   - c) Wait — down cows usually get up on their own
3. Pasture frothy bloat. Correct handling:
   - a) Run her to the barn
   - b) Walk her slowly with her head up, call the vet; a bloaty cow can die
     within an hour ✓
   - c) Feed hay to settle the stomach
